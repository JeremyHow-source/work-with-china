# 5 Reasons Why Chinese Companies Are Trusted Less Than Their Products Deserve

**Author:** Wenchao Wang  
**Published:** April 17, 2026  
**Link:** [Read on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/5-reasons-why-chinese-companies-trusted-less-than-products-wang-1dpqc)

---

In 2019, I sat across from a Chinese sales director in a hotel lobby in Kuala Lumpur.

He pulled out his phone and showed me a spec sheet. His company's product outperformed European competitors by two years in R&D. The price was 30% lower.

Their Southeast Asia revenue was still 70% behind their European rivals.

"Why?" I asked.

He was quiet for three seconds. Then: "Because they don't know if they can trust us."

That sentence has stayed with me ever since, because he wasn't talking about fraud or contracts.

He was talking about something harder to fix.

Here are 5 things I've learned about the real trust gap facing Chinese companies going global.

---

### ① Trust ≠ "I believe you won't cheat me"

In Western B2B culture, trust is built on two pillars: compliance and transparency.

Chinese companies going global have largely figured out compliance. Contracts, certifications, audits, they check the boxes.

But the second pillar — transparency — isn't about legal transparency. It's about cultural legibility.

Can an European or American partner, read your decision-making process? Can they understand why you said yes last Tuesday and changed direction on Thursday?

When I can't read the logic, I don't call it "fast." I call it unpredictable.

And unpredictable = untrustworthy.

---

### ② Speed destroys trust signals

Chinese companies are trained to move fast. "Say yes first, solve problems later" is a real operational philosophy, and in China, it works.

It signals agility, commitment, willingness.

In the West, it signals something else entirely.

A Dutch procurement manager told me: "When a Chinese supplier says yes to everything in the first meeting, my first thought is: they don't understand what they're agreeing to."

In Western negotiation culture, hesitation is a sign of seriousness. Pushback means you've actually read the contract. A quick yes feels like they're just trying to close the deal.

The same behavior. Opposite interpretation.

---

### ③ Localization ≠ Translation

Many Chinese companies think localization means hiring someone to translate the deck into English, put a local phone number on the website, and maybe add a smiling non-Chinese face to the homepage.

That's not localization. That's cosmetics.

Real localization means your thinking process is legible to the local market. It means your team knows why a British client needs two weeks to make a decision that your Chinese HQ would make in two hours — and doesn't interpret that as disinterest.

It means your case studies, your objection-handling, your after-sales structure — all of it maps onto how your target market thinks about risk, relationships, and accountability.

Most Chinese companies haven't done this work. Their English website exists. Their mindset hasn't shipped.

---

### ④ The missing trust proxy

Look at the most successful Chinese companies in global markets — DJI, Midea, BYD, Haier. Every single one of them has what I call a "bridge person."

Not just a translator. Not just a local sales hire.

A bridge person is someone who understands the internal Chinese rhythm (speed, hierarchy, decision flow) and carries genuine credibility in the local market. They can walk into a room with European clients and be believed. They can walk into an internal Chinese meeting and be understood.

These people are rare. Most Chinese companies either hire local staff who don't understand the Chinese side, or send Chinese staff who struggle to build authentic local credibility.

The gap between those two profiles is where trust goes to die.

---

### ⑤ The data gap is real — and it's not about the product

Research from CEIBS (China Europe International Business School) found that Chinese companies' brand trust scores in overseas markets average 47 percentage points lower than comparable European and American brands.

The product quality gap? Usually less than 10%.

You read that right. A 10% quality gap. A 47-point trust gap.

That asymmetry tells you everything. This isn't a product problem. It isn't a price problem. It isn't even a distribution problem.

It's a trust infrastructure problem.

---

### Final Thoughts

Chinese companies going global don't have a trust crisis. They have a trust infrastructure deficit.

The good news: infrastructure can be built. The bad news: it takes time, the right people, and a willingness to slow down before you can speed up.

The companies that crack this aren't the ones with the best product or the biggest marketing budget. They're the ones who invested in being readable to local partners, local hires, and local customers.

That's the real competitive advantage nobody talks about.
