# 4 Types of Global Professionals Who Can Work with China, and the Mistake Each One Makes

**Author:** Wenchao Wang  
**Published:** April 21, 2026  
**Link:** [Read on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/4-types-global-professionals-who-can-work-china-mistake-wenchao-wang-1943c)

---

In 2023, I sat between four people at a network event in Singapore.

One: a Chinese entrepreneur who moved to Sydney seven years ago. Two: a Singaporean who'd spent three years inside ByteDance. Three: a Dutch sales director who'd just returned from four years in Shanghai. Four: a British consultant who'd never set foot in China but had three Chinese clients.

All four were asking the same question: "How do I work better with Chinese companies?"

But they weren't talking about the same problem.

That's the moment I realized: the ability to Work with China isn't one thing. It's four very different starting points, with four very different blind spots.

Here's the matrix I've been using ever since.

---

### ① The Overseas Chinese Professional (Chinese background, working outside China)

This is the person everyone assumes has the advantage. They speak the language. They understand the culture. They know how family dinners work.

But here's the real challenge: both sides don't fully trust them.

Chinese teams in HQ sometimes treat them as "not quite Chinese anymore." Western colleagues sometimes assume they're "automatically on the Chinese side."

The typical mistake: assuming that cultural fluency from childhood still maps onto today's business reality. China's corporate world in 2024 is nothing like the China they grew up in — or left five, ten, twenty years ago.

The most dangerous phrase I hear from this group: "I know how Chinese people think."

Sometimes they do. Often, they're working from an outdated mental model.

---

### ② The Internationally-minded Chinese Professional (Based in China, with foreign company experience or overseas education)

This is the profile Chinese companies most actively recruit when going global. They've worked at a multinational. They speak English. They understand Western expectations.

But the classic trap: they become a human translation machine rather than a decision-maker.

They translate the deck. They explain the Chinese boss's logic to the foreign partner. They soften the hard message. They bridge the meeting.

And then they go home exhausted, with no seat at the table.

The mistake isn't skill. It's positioning. They let themselves be used as a relay station instead of insisting on being the architect of the strategy.

---

### ③ The Foreigner Already in China (Living and working inside the country)

Being physically present is a real advantage — context, relationships, speed of response. These people understand the pace. They've been in the WeChat groups. They know that a contract signed on Friday can be renegotiated on Monday.

But the brutal truth: if their Mandarin doesn't clear a certain threshold, they remain an outsider with an insider's schedule.

I've met foreign executives in Shanghai who'd lived there for six years and still couldn't read the room in a Chinese meeting. Not because they weren't smart — but because the unspoken signals, the hierarchy cues, the face-saving redirects all happen in a frequency they couldn't tune into.

Presence without language access is living in a foreign country inside an expat bubble.

---

### ④ The Foreigner Who Has Never Been to China (Working with Chinese companies from outside, often in Europe, North America, Southeast Asia)

This is the most underestimated group.

The assumption is: they don't know enough to be effective. They'll get lost in cultural misunderstandings. They need to "go experience China first."

Here's my contrarian view: for Chinese companies going global, the outside perspective is exactly what's needed.

A German logistics partner who's never been to China but knows European supply chain law cold? That's not a liability. That's leverage — if they position it correctly.

The real mistake this group makes isn't ignorance. It's paralysis. They spend six months doing research before making a single call. They wait until they feel "ready." They never feel ready.

The entry barrier here isn't knowledge. It's confidence in the value they already have.

---

### The Rarest Profile of All

Across all four types, the person who actually unlocks the most value isn't the one who knows China best.

It's the one who can be trusted by a Chinese boss and read by a Western partner at the same time, in the same room.

I call this "bilingual credibility." Not language. Credibility.

It means your Chinese counterpart believes you understand what's at stake for them. And your Western counterpart believes you're not just echoing the party line.

This person is extraordinarily rare. Most people optimize for one side and sacrifice the other.

---

### Final Thoughts

The mistake most people make when trying to Work with China is using the wrong map.

They ask: "How do I understand China better?" when the more useful question is: "What specific value can I offer — given exactly where I'm starting from?"

Your starting point isn't a disadvantage. It's your entry angle.

The best Work with China professionals aren't the most knowledgeable about China.

They're the ones who've figured out how to make China readable, to the people on the other side of the table.

That's a skill. It can be built. But only if you know which of the four types you actually are.
