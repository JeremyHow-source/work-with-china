# I Evaluate Chinese Companies the Same Way I Pick Watermelons

**Author:** Wenchao Wang  
**Published:** June 15, 2026  
**Link:** [Read on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/i-evaluate-chinese-companies-same-way-pick-watermelons-wenchao-wang-lsr9c)

---

Two years ago, a Singaporean friend asked me a question I get all the time:

How do you know if a Chinese company is actually worth joining?

He was considering an offer from a mid-sized Chinese firm. Good package. But something felt off.

He actually had three follow-up questions:

1. Is this company actually solid, or just looks solid from the outside?
2. Can I build real trust there, as an outsider?
3. And where exactly is my ceiling?

Good sharp questions.

I walked him through six things I check whenever I'm doing due diligence on a company:

1. What do the financials actually look like?
2. Is the founder's direction consistent, or does it shift every six months?
3. Does the title mean anything?
4. Are the equity and options real, or just there to make the offer look bigger?
5. Did the top person meet you personally? For roles that actually matter, founders want to see you themselves.
6. What are the cultural signals that aren't in the contract? Some things you only find out after you're inside.

---

He frowned.

"That's a full due diligence checklist," he said. "Too many checks."

He knew he could do this kind of work with my help. But he wanted to know if there was a faster way, something simpler.

I smiled, and asked him: Are you looking for a shortcut?

He shook his head. No. He said he always felt like there should be one quick way to see through to the core of a company. Was he wrong?

I said: actually, there is one solution for fast diagnosis.

Then I told him about how I pick watermelons. He got it immediately.

---

## The Watermelon Method

Every summer in Australia, supermarkets stack watermelons outside in big piles.

When I go to pick one, strangers stop to watch me.

I hold the watermelon up close to my ear, knock on it once with my knuckle, and listen.

A deep, hollow, resonant thud — ripe. It can be juicy and sweet.

A high, tight sound, like knocking on something hard — not ready.

They always ask me: what are you doing? Can you actually tell?

I tell them: you don't need to cut it open. Knock once. The sound travels through the whole thing and comes back. Whatever's inside, the echo tells you. It's not perfect every time, but seven or eight out of ten, the one I walk away with is good.

Reading a company works the same way.

Sometimes you need to go deep, item by item, building certainty layer by layer.

But sometimes, one knock is enough. You don't need to cut the whole thing open. You knock in the right place, hear what comes back, and you already know.

---

## So Where Do You Knock?

The people at the top. Senior leadership, and the shareholders behind them.

If you check a good amount of Chinese mid-sized companies and you'll find very similar pattern: the CFO is owner's wife, the COO is a cousin, the people controlling supply chain and procurement are all family.

This is a signal.

Not that the company is bad — many of them run well. It means their trust travels along bloodlines. As an outsider, you need to understand: there may be a door that stays closed to you, no matter how good you are.

But if you see a different pattern, that's a different echo.

Professional executives who aren't relatives. Old university friends of the founder. Early investors who were classmates. A trust circle that has expanded beyond family.

That's a good signal.

It means this founder has learned to turn people outside the family into insiders. A company like that might actually have a seat for someone who earns their way in on merit.

---

## Pop Mart: A Case Study

Pop Mart is that kind of company.

Founder Wang Ning — Peking University Guanghua MBA.

COO Si De — same program, same cohort.

CFO Yang Jingbing — also came through there.

But the one worth stopping on is someone else entirely.

The person running Pop Mart's entire international business is a Korean national named Moon Deok. His path into the company also ran through a Peking University MBA. Today, he sits on the board as an executive director, inside the innermost circle of a Chinese company.

This is all public. POP MART is listed in Hong Kong. Names, backgrounds, titles — it's all in their disclosure filings.

A foreigner made it into the core circle of a Chinese company.

That fact alone is the echo that comes back when you knock.

---

## So How Do You Get Into That Kind of Trust Circle?

My Singaporean friend heard all of this, then turned it around on me:

"Okay. So how do I get into that kind of trust circle?"

I said: flip the question.

This Korean guy did it. Why couldn't you? He found a path in. That path still exists.

Have you considered doing an EMBA in China? Joint programs between Chinese and foreign universities are still open. That door hasn't closed.

One warning though: these programs vary wildly. Pay attention to who's in the room with you. Some cohorts are full of professional managers working for large corporations. That's a different circle from the one you want.

If your Mandarin is strong, go for a Chinese-language cohort. That's where you actually build the relationships that matter.

If your Mandarin isn't there yet, choose programs with more entrepreneurs, not academics. Peking University, Zhejiang University, programs where more people have started their own thing. More academic programs skew toward corporate managers. Know which one fits your goal.

---

At the end of that conversation, he asked me: so what's the answer? What should I do?

I told him: I don't have a formula.

But an MBA/EMBA in China is worth considering, isn't it?
