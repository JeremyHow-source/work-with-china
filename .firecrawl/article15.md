Recently, several new connections on LinkedIn sent me messages with a similar curiosity. They looked at my profile, saw my focus area, and asked:

"What exactly do you do? What does it mean by 'Work with China'? Who are you actually talking about?"

It’s a fair question.

For many, the immediate reaction is geographical: "Do you mean working physically in China?"

In the reality of cross-cultural practice, "Work with China" has never been just a geographical concept. It is a relationship concept.

After spending the first ten years of my career in China, growing up there, but working professionally for multinational companies from the West. I’ve come to see this through three distinct dimensions of relationships.

1\. Work for Chinese companies

This isn't limited to being in Beijing or Shanghai. It includes international talents working for Chinese enterprises globally, whether in an overseas branch, a remote collaboration, or a cross-border team. The essence here is entering the internal operating system of a Chinese organization.

2\. Study in China

This group isn't immediately in the workforce, but they are entering the ecosystem through an academic or research path. Exchange students, researchers, or participants in short-term industrial programs. On the surface, they are "studying," but in reality, they are building a long-term cognitive framework of China’s society and business environment.

3\. Partner with Chinese companies

This is often the most overlooked but statistically significant group. Suppliers, clients, channel partners, joint venture stakeholders, or co-founders. You might never receive a paycheck from a Chinese company, but your business survival depends on continuous, high-stakes interaction with them.

### The Common Thread: Real Connection

What do these three paths share? It’s not their location. It’s the texture of their connection.

They are all engaged in concrete, continuous, and irreplaceable interactions with China. They are past the stage of being "interested" or just reading news headlines. They are in the arena.

Because of this, I’ve observed a fascinating phenomenon. Whether you are an employee, a student, or a partner, the anxieties you face are similar.

In my conversations with international professionals, we almost always circle back to the same set of underlying questions:

- How does the business environment actually function beneath the surface?
- How do cultural nuances impact critical communication?
- What are the unspoken rules of social engagement?
- How do cities differ in opportunity and temperament?
- What is the logic behind the systems and processes?
- The reality of safety and lifestyle convenience.
- The sustainability of a career path involved with China.
- And simply, the authentic experience of daily life.

### My Approach: Perspective, Not Propaganda

Before we go further in this newsletter series, I want to be transparent about my vantage point.

I was born and raised in China. My worldview is shaped by the rapid changes of the last few decades. However, my professional training was largely within Western MNCs operating in China. This gives me a specific lens: I am an insider to the culture, but often an observer of the local corporate machinery.

This means my writing will inevitably carry my personal subjectivity and biases. I cannot promise to be the "source of all truth."

What I can promise is this: I will remain neutral within the scope of my cognition. I will not sugarcoat the difficulties of the Chinese market, nor will I engage in ideological or political debates.

My goal is to provide a coordinate system for those who want to play the long game.

### The Roadmap Ahead

Moving forward, this newsletter will systematically explore the eight dimensions mentioned above:

1. Business Environment: Beyond the GDP numbers.
2. Cultural Differences: The friction points that actually matter.
3. Social Norms: The hidden rules of trust-building.
4. City Selection: Why Shanghai is not Beijing, and Shenzhen is neither.
5. System Logic: Understanding the "Why" behind the bureaucracy.
6. Safety & Security: The psychological versus the physical.
7. Career Path: Is "China Expert" still a valid career tag?
8. Real Life Experience: The texture of living there.

To me, "Work with China" is not a job description. It is a long-term mode of engagement. It’s about building a capability that stays with you, regardless of where your desk is located.

I’m curious to hear from you: Does your experience fit into one of the three categories I described? Or does "Work with China" mean something entirely different to you?
