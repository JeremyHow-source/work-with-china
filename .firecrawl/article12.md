![I've Seen Hundreds of Chinese Bosses. Here's the Quickest Way to Tell the Good Ones.](https://media.licdn.com/dms/image/v2/D5612AQGjaFih7eBV_w/article-cover_image-shrink_720_1280/B56Z0aQU51IMAI-/0/1774261989735?e=2147483647&v=beta&t=lqCnX-4X6AQnMizVU6-zK-fnecN2OSvOUrHoleyNBpg)

A sales manager in Hangzhou did Chinese RMB 100 million in revenue in a single year during COVID by selling protective equipment.

When I heard this story, it wasn't from the sales manager herself.

It was from her boss. And the way he told it? He couldn't stop smiling.

That moment stuck with me. Because in my experience, how a boss talks about their people tells you almost everything you need to know.

* * *

I have a friend who spent over two decades as a senior executive in multinationals before joining a Chinese private company. He shared an observation that I keep coming back to.

In the MNC world, he had a simple rule for reading leaders:

The good ones say "Follow me." The average ones say "Go."

One walks alongside the team. The other sends people in and watches from behind.

Simple. And it worked — for twenty years.

But when he joined a Chinese company, that rule stopped working.

* * *

Here's why.

Many Chinese business founders built their companies from scratch, with their own hands. Being hands-on isn't a weakness — it's often how they survived. So "Follow me" can mean genuine leadership, or it can just mean a founder who can't let go.

What he noticed instead was something more telling: what the boss talks about when they really get going.

He saw two kinds of bosses.

The first kind lights up when talking about themselves — their early hustle, the deals they closed, the obstacles they overcame. Ask them about their team? They give you a name or two. Move on quickly.

The second kind mentions their own story almost in passing. But ask about their people — and suddenly they're leaning forward, full of details, genuinely proud.

He told me: following the first kind is exhausting. Not because the work is hard. Because you're always reading the room, watching their mood, managing up.

Following the second kind? You actually have room to grow.

I've seen this play out up close.

A founder I know in Shenzhen — every time we meet, he leads with his team. Recently it was a sales rep who closed several major accounts within six months, worth tens of millions in RMB. He described it in detail. You'd think he was talking about his own kid acing an exam.

Another founder in Shanghai told me about an employee who never seemed that busy — but consistently topped the monthly sales rankings. When he looked closer, he found out why: this person had built up nearly 5,000 targeted connections on LinkedIn over the years. Quietly. Methodically. Long before anyone else thought to.

The Shanghai founder told me this with real admiration in his voice.

Both of these bosses? Their people tend to stick around. And they tend to do well.

* * *

So if you're a global professional considering working with or for a Chinese company, here's my honest suggestion:

Don't just evaluate the company. Sit down with the boss. And ask them two questions:

1\. "How did you build the business?"2\. "Who on your team are you most proud of — and why?"

Then listen carefully.

If they talk about themselves with passion but go vague when it comes to their people — take your time before deciding.

If they gloss over their own story but come alive when they talk about someone on their team — that's a boss worth following.

* * *

The best leaders I've observed aren't the ones with the most impressive personal story.

They're the ones who get more excited about your story than their own.

That's not a small thing. That's the whole thing.

* * *
