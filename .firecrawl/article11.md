My Australian friend Adam's company got acquired by a Chinese tech giant.

$40 million deal.

Adam survived the layoffs. He kept his job.

Six weeks later, he flew to Guangzhou for "orientation week" at the new Chinese headquarters.

He came back a different person.

* * *

### The Story Adam Told Me

"I walked in at 8:55 AM on Monday," Adam told me. "Feeling ready to hit the ground running. 15 years experience. Survived the acquisition. I'm thinking: let me show them what I can do."

The office was already full.

Not just working. Deep in flow.

His manager Chen greeted him warmly. "Adam! Welcome! This first week, just observe. Don't try to add value yet. Just watch, listen, learn."

Adam was confused. "I'm here to help. I have ideas."

Chen smiled. "I know. But you're in a new country with new rules. Let me teach you the rules before you play the game."

"In Western companies, you show up and immediately show what you can do."

"In Chinese companies, you show up and first show that you respect how we do things."

By Wednesday, Adam couldn't resist. He spoke up in a meeting: "I think we could streamline this..."

The room went quiet.

That evening, a colleague told him: "In Chinese companies, we judge character first. Skills second."

Adam didn't understand then.

He does now.

* * *

## 3 Things You SHALL Do

### 1\. Bring Something From Home

Adam brought nothing but his laptop.

His Chinese colleagues brought Australian wine, specialty coffee, local snacks.

They weren't just being nice.

What Adam learned:

In Chinese business culture, gifts aren't gifts. They're relationship tokens.

Tim Tams from Melbourne say: "I thought about you before I came."

What Adam does now: • Brings small gifts (tim tams, wine, coffee) • Shares personal stories when giving them

The insight: Westerners: "Professional relationships should be transactional." Chinese reality: "All relationships are personal first."

* * *

### 2\. Remember Chinese Names (Not Just English Ones)

Everyone introduced themselves with English names. "I'm Kevin." "I'm Fiona."

Adam used English names exclusively.

Then he noticed: The CEO always used Chinese names.

What Adam learned:

Using someone's English name is fine. Using their Chinese name says: "I respect you enough to learn."

What Adam does now: • Asks: "How do you write your name in Chinese?" • Practices pronunciation (badly, but tries) • Uses Chinese names in one-on-one conversations

The insight: Language isn't just communication. It's a signal of respect.

* * *

### 3\. Show Up to "Optional" Social Events

Week 1, team dinner on Thursday. Adam was tired. Wanted to skip.

"Surely it's optional," he thought.

Almost skipped.

Then he remembered: "When in Rome..."

What he discovered:

The dinner wasn't optional in any meaningful sense.

Important information was shared. Relationships were built. He learned more in 3 hours than 3 days of meetings.

What Adam does now: • Never skips team dinners, drinks, social events • Treats social time as work time (because it is)

The insight: You can opt out. But you opt yourself out of information flow, relationships, and trust.

## 2 Things To AVOID

### 1\. Don't Say "Back At My Old Company..."

Adam said this 3 times in week one.

Each time: polite smile → conversation moved on.

What he learned:

You think: "Sharing best practices."

They hear: "My old way was better. Your way is backward."

What Adam does now: • Asks "How do you handle this here?" instead of suggesting changes • Shares experiences as stories, not recommendations

* * *

### 2\. Don't Try To "Fix" Things In Month One

Adam saw inefficiencies everywhere. He's a process expert. Couldn't resist pointing them out.

People stopped listening.

Not because he was wrong. But timing matters more than correctness.

What he learned:

You can't walk into someone's house and immediately rearrange the furniture.

First, prove you respect the house.

What Adam does now: • Waits 3 months before suggesting major changes • Starts with "I'm curious..." not "Have you considered..."

* * *

## 2 Things to WATCH For

### 1\. The "Voluntary" Overtime (That's Not Mandatory)

Adam noticed by day three: Work ended at 6:00 PM, but most people stayed. 7:00. 8:00. Sometimes later.

He asked: "Is this mandatory?"

His colleague laughed. "No one's forcing us."

Then why do they stay?

What he discovered:

Staying late isn't just about work. It's about: • Social bonding: Team dinner together • Signal of commitment: "I'm here when the team needs me" • Career currency: Who shows up gets noticed

It's not forced labor. It's voluntary participation in a team ritual.

The insight: What Westerners see as "exploitation," many Chinese employees see as "community."

* * *

### 2\. The Meeting Silence (That Means Agreement, Not Confusion)

Adam's second week, strategy meeting. CEO presented a plan. No one spoke. No questions.

Adam thought: "Everyone's confused. I should clarify."

He raised his hand. "I have some questions."

The room went cold.

After the meeting, his colleague pulled him aside: "In Western meetings, silence means you don't understand. In Chinese meetings, silence often means we understand and we agree."

The reverse-shock:

Westerners think Chinese meetings are "passive."

Chinese professionals think Western meetings are "chaotic" (everyone interrupting, challenging, debating).

The insight: Western efficiency: "Get everything on the table now." Chinese harmony: "Maintain the relationship first, solve problems privately later."

* * *

### What Adam Says Now

Six months later, Adam is thriving.

He didn't become "Chinese." He learned a new operating system.

"The companies that figure this out first? They're going to win."
