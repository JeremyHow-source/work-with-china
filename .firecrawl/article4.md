![5 Things the GoPro Story Tells You About Working for a Chinese Company](https://media.licdn.com/dms/image/v2/D4E12AQHRkAzTGyiQWg/article-cover_image-shrink_720_1280/B4EZ5dNzT8I8AQ-/0/1779680364328?e=2147483647&v=beta&t=yvJVqhc9S6hklUOhWUA1kAwKJhpG71jzjHXrRNTmKQ4)

GoPro is for sale.

Not the cameras. The whole company.

Last week, GoPro announced a strategic review — evaluating all possibilities including sale and merger. Its market cap today: 13 billion.

That's a 98.7% decline.

Meanwhile, Insta360's flagship camera now costs $559. GoPro's costs $329.

The Chinese brand charges more. Holds a bigger market share. And GoPro can't figure out how to compete.

I've been watching this shift for years. And I want to tell you what it actually means — not for investors, but for your career.

Here are 5 things the GoPro story is telling you.

### 1\. "Cost advantage" is the story people stopped updating

People still assume Chinese companies win because they're cheap. That stopped being true a long time ago.

Eight years ago, Insta360 had revenues roughly one-fortieth of GoPro's. Both shared many of the same core suppliers. Similar starting point. But by Q1-Q3 2025, Insta360's revenue exceeded $900M. GoPro's was less than a third of that. And falling.

GoPro's gross margin has collapsed from 32.1% to 4.3%. They are now losing money on every unit they sell.

How did this happen? Insta360 releases meaningful updates every few months. AI-powered video editing. Automatic reframing. 8K resolution. Each one solving a problem users were actually complaining about. GoPro does the same things. Just slower. And now at a lower price.

The real competitive edge isn't cost. It's the speed at which Chinese companies turn user feedback into product cycles.

### 2\. Speed changes what kind of people they need to hire

If the advantage were purely cheap manufacturing, they wouldn't need foreign professionals. They'd just hire cheaper.

When Xiaomi opened a European R&D center in Munich in 2025, they hired Fabian Schmölz — the designer who led the Lamborghini Temerario and previously worked on the Porsche Taycan. Rudolf Dittrich joined from BMW's M4 GT3 program. Julien Cueff came from Mercedes-Benz.

These are not diversity hires. These are people brought in because the product needs to meet the expectations of the world's most demanding buyers — from the design language down to the engineering specs. Getting it right in Europe isn't separate from getting it right everywhere. It's the same product.

### 3\. The window is structural, not cyclical

In 2025, China's total outbound direct investment reached $174.4 billion — up 7.1% from the year before. That's 11,048 overseas enterprises operating across 153 countries.

Each one needs people who can run it. Every overseas office, every partnership, every local team.

The companies doing this aren't just Huawei, BYD, and ByteDance. The real volume is in the layer below: tens of thousands of companies pulling in 200M a year, right now moving into Europe, Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and North America. Most people have never heard of them. Some of them are paying $500K for a single VP.

### 4\. Most foreign professionals measure themselves against the wrong criteria

I've sat across from too many people who spent the interview listing all the ways they weren't good enough. Mandarin not fluent. Never worked in China. Don't know the market.

They were measuring themselves against criteria the company wasn't actually using.

Chinese companies already have entire floors of Chinese employees. They don't need one more person who is almost, but not quite, Chinese. They need the person you already are — the local relationships, the cultural instincts, the ability to walk into a room in Berlin or Dubai and be treated as an insider from the first handshake.

The things you think of as completely ordinary about yourself may be exactly what they're missing.

### 5\. The people who moved early built something real

I know someone who joined Huawei more than ten years ago. Everyone around him said the move was too risky.

He used the relationships and understanding he built there to found a PR and government relations firm for Chinese companies going global. Many Chinese company operating overseas has worked with him at some point.

He bet on the window at the right time.

Final Thoughts: GoPro's story isn't about one company losing to another. It's about an entire industry logic shifting underneath everyone's feet: from recording device to content production tool, and one side moving fast enough to catch it while the other didn't. That same shift is happening across industries right now. The question isn't whether Chinese companies are winning. The question is whether you're positioned to be part of how they do it.

What problem can you solve that they can't solve from the inside?
