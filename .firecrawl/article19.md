![6 Questions You Need to Ask Before Joining a Chinese Company](https://media.licdn.com/dms/image/v2/D5612AQFeI-BWdLhDWQ/article-cover_image-shrink_720_1280/B56ZqWatGtH8AM-/0/1763460178949?e=2147483647&v=beta&t=zJ4pFveCIFokb7iUj9Sq7fef8Im1nDsMAll8-9qa46M)

My Australian friend Kate joined a Chinese multinational four years ago. She needed an $800 laptop, but approval took one month. Because it was their first time hiring a foreign employee, and the laptop price exceeded their internal China budget. Two months later, Kate resigned.

This isn’t an isolated story. Many foreign executives join Chinese companies without asking the right questions—leading to costly misunderstandings.

Here are six key questions you definitely need to ask before signing the contract with your overseas employer in China.

### ① The Company Looks Big — But How Stable Is It?

You might think the company is “big,” but the truth is—they’re often just “growing fast.”

Many Chinese companies grow by sacrificing margin for scale. Fast growth, weak cash flow.

Common mistake: “They’re funded, visible, and expanding—must be safe, right?”

Well, maybe not. In China, the biggest companies are often the most fragile.

So check below facts:

What’s the gross margin?

Are they truly profitable?

How long can they survive if their biggest market or client turns?

### ② Does the company have a “boss-led culture”

Many foreigners assume “boss-led culture” in China means unprofessional or unsophisticated—and they want to “fix it.”

Reality check: In many Chinese companies, processes are just delayed expressions of the boss’s emotions.

You’ll see KPIs launched today... and killed tomorrow. 5-page process charts on the table, but everyone follows the boss’s mood instead.

Well, I am not saying all Chinese companies would be like that. Smart leaders like Xiaomi’s Lei Jun or ByteDance’s Zhang Yiming know how to delegate and control themselves.

So in order to aim for companies like theirs, you'd better ask:

How does the founder make decisions?

Can they restrain their impulses?

### ③ You Have a Title. But Are You Sure You Will Get the Power?

Many foreign VPs in Chinese companies have fancy titles—but need approval for a $500 purchase.

So make sure you ask:

Who signs off on spending?

Which department actually holds power?

Who really makes decisions in the chain of command for the function you are heading?

Don’t trust the org chart. Follow the PO approvals.

### ④ Comp Package Sounds Big — But Does the Pie Have Edges?

Chinese companies are great at selling “future upside.” Some call it equity. I call it hope.

Common pitch: “We’ll IPO soon—your rewards will multiply.”

What if they don’t IPO? What if there are no shares, no bonus, no cash—but they still expect you to act like an “owner”?

So you'd better ask yourself before joining:

Do the stock options actually exist?

What’s the vesting period?

Is your base salary solid enough to protect you?

### ⑤ Did the CEO Meet You? It’s Not About Face — It’s About Fate.

If you’re EVP, VP, or C-suite and the CEO doesn’t want to meet you?

This can already say that the role isn’t important, and you won’t be supported. Or no one is willing to sponsor your success.

So during the interview:

What does the CEO expect from this role?

Who truly initiated the hire?

Are you a key part of their future plans?

### ⑥ Values & Trust: Written on the Wall vs. Written in the Heart

Like companies everywhere, Chinese companies also list “values” on walls and websites.

But real trust often comes from family-style or brother-style culture.

It’s not easy to see this before joining, but you can ask smart questions like:

How do you handle internal disagreements on big decisions?

As a foreign leader, how can I earn trust quickly here?

In the West, culture is written in the handbook. In China, culture is written on the boss’s face.

In the West, processes exist because trust is low. In China, processes exist because things move too fast.

The biggest mistake Western executives make in China is not underestimating speed—but overestimating their own importance.

If you’re considering joining a Chinese multinational, these six questions are your pre-flight checklist.
