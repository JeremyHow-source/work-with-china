A friend of mine on LinkedIn works for a large state-owned enterprise in the Middle East.

Good salary. Long contract. Stable position.

One day he messaged me: "I'm done with everything by noon. Then I just wait. Nobody really needs my opinion. I feel like a bolt in a machine, just spinning in place."

He now realized he walked into the wrong type of company.

This is the mistake I see most often. And it happens before the interview even starts.

"Chinese companies" covers four entirely different species. They operate in the same language, in the same country — but their hiring logic, pay structures, and attitudes toward foreign talent are meaningfully different. Get the type right, and things go smoother than you'd expect. Get it wrong, and you spend two years confused.

Here's how I break them down.

* * *

① State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs)

SOEs are owned or majority-controlled by the government. Energy, infrastructure, banking, telecom, power — companies of staggering scale. Well-resourced and stable.

But an SOE operates on a different logic than a market-driven company. Decisions travel through layers. Seniority matters more than immediate results. At a Western company, your position is clear: your manager tells you, your numbers tell you. At an SOE, that feedback loop is much harder to read.

Large organizations, especially SOEs, navigate gray areas. Problems get handled when they surface, not before. Progress happens until it doesn't. For a foreigner used to clear rules and defined processes, this is the hardest thing to adapt to.

My friend in the Middle East wasn't dealing with a failing company. He finished his work by noon and waited. He didn't know for what, or how long. For someone used to a results-driven environment, that kind of waiting is suffocating.

SOEs hire foreigners in two main situations: highly specialized technical roles that can't be filled domestically, or Belt and Road Initiative projects overseas that need local-facing staff. One large central SOE building a cultural facility in Riyadh offered $70,000/year for a project manager role. Same background, same title, at a private company going global — double that isn't unusual. SOEs aren't lowballing. Their salary structures require multiple approvals. There isn't much flexibility built in.

* * *

② Private Companies

Private companies are where foreigners have the most opportunities. They move fast, they're willing to pay for talent they actually need, and they're not backed by the government — which means they survive on results.

But "private companies" covers too much ground. Putting Huawei and a ten-person Shenzhen startup in the same category doesn't help you decide anything. So we need to break this down.

Large private companies — Huawei, ByteDance, BYD, Alibaba, CATL. Their global push has created direct demand for foreign talent. ByteDance built a US data security team of more than 2,500 people. BYD's European operations team includes employees from 40 countries. None of this is diversity optics — it's the staffing structure that emerges when the business scales.

But many of them don't have a great reputation overseas. TikTok's Glassdoor work-life balance score in the US is 2.5 out of 5. One employee's words kept appearing in reviews: "No matter how high you climb, the people above you are always Chinese." They're expanding internationally far faster than they're building cross-cultural management capability. That gap is exactly where you can make your mark — if you understand it going in.

Mid-sized private companies — $50M–$200M in annual revenue, pushing into global markets, teams of a few hundred to a few thousand. No name recognition. But they need you more, and they're more willing to give you real responsibility.

A large private company is like a chain restaurant: the menu is fixed, the output is consistent, but changing the menu is nearly impossible. A mid-sized private company is more like a restaurant that opened five years ago, built a loyal customer base, and is now thinking about a second location. The owner is still in the kitchen every day. You mention an idea for a new dish, and it might be on the menu next week.

I've worked with two companies that illustrate this. One is a fashion accessories trading company in Shenzhen, doing around $200M a year, with customers across Europe — hiring foreign staff for over a decade. The other is based in Shanghai, $100M a year. The owner called me asking for a VP of Business Development. Opening offer: $500,000 base salary.

Mid-sized private companies share one characteristic: their overseas business is validated, but the local team to sustain it hasn't been built yet. That gap is your entry point.

My advice: when you're young, start at a large private company. The brand is real value. Once you have that first stretch of Chinese company experience on your resume, move to a mid-sized private. Faster promotions. Decisions closer to you. You can see your work matter directly.

③ Wholly Foreign-Owned Enterprises (WFOEs)

Apple, IKEA, Tesla in China. For foreigners who want to build China experience without jumping fully into a Chinese company, this is a practical stepping stone. You're in a real Chinese market environment, but your management chain and company culture still feel familiar.

The total number of positions at foreign companies in China has been shrinking — that's a clear trend. But if your goal is to first establish a track record in China, a WFOE is worth serious consideration as a starting point.

* * *

④ Joint Ventures

A joint venture is jointly owned by a Chinese party and a foreign party. SAIC Volkswagen, Beijing Benz — these are typical examples.

This is the hardest category to characterize. The power structures in JVs are often complicated. A JV where the foreign side holds control looks completely different from one where the Chinese side is in charge.

Before you join any joint venture, one question is worth asking directly before anything else: Who actually calls the shots here?

I know of a case — 70% Chinese ownership, 30% foreign. Governance and decision-making authority were clearly documented before signing. Then the company started operating, and reality looked nothing like the paperwork. The Chinese chairman stepped directly into day-to-day decisions. The foreign general manager's authority was gradually hollowed out. He couldn't make calls. He couldn't change direction. The project eventually ran into serious trouble.

Nothing was wrong with the structure itself. What was wrong was that both sides had different understandings of who was in charge — and nobody resolved that before it became a problem.

Figure that out before you sign anything.

* * *

So how do you choose?

One question cuts through everything: What do you need right now — credentials, or room to run?

No track record in a Chinese company yet → large private. The brand credential becomes the foundation for everything after.

Already have experience, want faster decisions and bigger responsibility → mid-sized private.

Want China market exposure inside a familiar structure → WFOE.

Drawn to a specific industry where Chinese and foreign players collaborate → JV, but figure out who runs things first.

SOEs have their place: Belt and Road infrastructure, specialized technical roles, or anyone who genuinely doesn't mind trading speed for stability. Just be honest with yourself about what you're trading.

The type you pick shapes everything that follows. Most people only figure this out after they've already joined.

遇上「王伦型上司」：职场中的隐形天花板

在职场中，每个人都渴望被赏识、被提拔、被委以重任。但如果你才华横溢、心怀愿景，却恰好遇上了一位典型的「王伦型上司」，你该如何自处？

所谓 “王伦型上司”，是指那些才识平平却身居高位、心胸狭窄又妒贤嫉能、害怕被超越而刻意打压有能力下属的人。他们不重愿景、不谈价值观，只关注自己的地位是否受到威胁。在《水浒传》中，王伦看见林冲的履历，非但不欢迎，反劝其另谋高就，正是这种领导心态的真实写照。

第一层判断：识局而不盲动
面临王伦型上司，第一步不是反抗，而是 "识局"。你要判断的是：
• 公司是否整体文化支持王伦式领导？
• 高层是否意识到王伦的局限？
• 自己是否有可转移的平台或资源？

如果整个组织都是 “王伦文化”，内斗成风、打压人才、忽视愿景，那你需要冷静地认清现实：这不是你的舞台。与其死守不适合自己的平台，不如筹谋第二曲线。

结语：良禽择木而栖，贤臣择主而事，见机不早悔之晚矣
