![5 Things People Get Wrong About "Working with China", and Why "Going to China" Is the Wrong First Step](https://media.licdn.com/dms/image/v2/D5612AQFqTXu80HV4qg/article-cover_image-shrink_720_1280/B56Z1rY6FUGoAI-/0/1775623193538?e=2147483647&v=beta&t=Uu5M5W-4fvN6JtNqwql7ESRzKBYt382tdQtT-ROhZJg)

A German mechanical engineer friend of mine has never set foot in China. Not once. Yet for the past two years, he's been the European market consultant for a drone company headquartered in Shenzhen, helping them navigate EU regulatory frameworks, build distributor relationships in Germany and the Netherlands, and translate product specs into language that German industrial buyers actually trust.

The Shenzhen team's European revenue tripled. He's never needed a visa.

This isn't an isolated story.

Here's what most people get wrong: they treat "Work with China" as a geography problem. As in — you need to be there to make it work. But the real question isn't where you are. It's what kind of relationship you've built.

Here are 5 things that actually define whether you can work with China — and location is barely on the list.

* * *

### 1 \| Most people think "Work with China" means going to China.

The mental image is still: fly to Beijing, shake hands, get the deal done.

But the reality is that today, over 500,000 foreign professionals interact daily with Chinese companies in some capacity — and a significant portion of them have never lived in China.

They're consultants based in Singapore, lawyers in London, engineers in Stuttgart, marketing directors in São Paulo.

The Chinese logic here is actually straightforward: results matter more than presence.

If you can deliver what's needed — market access, technical credibility, regulatory expertise. Chinese business partners will work with you remotely.

What they won't tolerate is someone who shows up physically but brings no real value.

Geography creates proximity. It doesn't create trust.

* * *

### 2 \| "Work with China" is not one thing. It's three different relationships.

Most people lump everything together. But there are actually three distinct modes, and each has its own cultural entry point:

Work for Chinese companies (you're an employee or contractor inside a Chinese org structure). This requires understanding hierarchy, decision speed, and the unspoken rule that your boss's face matters as much as your output.

Study in or learn from China (you're building knowledge — language, market, culture). This is the longest runway but builds the deepest roots. The people who studied in China in the 2000s are now the most valuable cross-cultural talent on the market.

Partner with Chinese companies (you're an external collaborator — supplier, distributor, advisor). This is where the German engineer lives. The trust logic is different: you earn it through competence first, relationship second. Not the other way around.

Mixing up which mode you're in is one of the most common mistakes I see.

A "partner" who behaves like an "employee" will confuse everyone. A consultant who tries to operate like a student will lose credibility fast.

* * *

### 3 \| Remote collaboration with China isn't a pandemic hangover, it's becoming the default.

Many people assume that post-COVID remote work with China is a temporary compromise. That eventually, things will go back to "normal", meaning, in-person.

But the Chinese companies I work with have quietly upgraded their remote infrastructure in ways that make physical presence less necessary than ever.

WeChat Work, Feishu (Lark), and internal video systems have matured. Decision-making documentation has improved. And frankly, Chinese companies going global need local experts who stay local, because that's where their customers are.

In 2024, I helped connect a Hangzhou-based EdTech company with a Southeast Asia curriculum advisor based in Kuala Lumpur. She works entirely remotely.

Two years in, she's become their de facto product localization lead for Malaysia and Indonesia. She flies to Hangzhou once a year — for the relationship, not the work.

* * *

### 4 \| What Chinese companies actually need isn't language or cultural performance. It's someone who can hold two rhythms at once.

Many westerners prepare for China by learning Mandarin or reading books on "face" and "guanxi." That's not wrong. But it misses the deeper thing Chinese partners are evaluating.

Chinese companies move fast. Decisions that take 3 months in a European corporate can happen in 3 weeks in Shenzhen. But relationship-building moves slow, trust accumulates through consistency, small gestures, and showing up when it's inconvenient.

The people who thrive in China collaboration are those who can hold both rhythms simultaneously. They can sprint on deliverables and be patient on relationships. They don't confuse urgency with intimacy.

In the West, we tend to separate the two: business is fast, relationships take time. In China, the relationship is the business — but it operates on its own clock, separate from the project timeline.

The German engineer I mentioned? He sends WeChat voice messages to his Shenzhen counterpart every Friday — not about work, just checking in.

It takes 90 seconds. It signals: I'm here, I'm consistent, I care. That's worth more than any business trip.

* * *

### 5 \| The people winning in China collaboration aren't the ones who know China best. They're the ones China trusts most.

This is the most counterintuitive one.

I've seen foreign executives with 15 years in Shanghai fail to build meaningful partnerships because they came across as overconfident, or subtly condescending — "I know China, I've been here forever."

And I've seen a 28-year-old Australian with two years of WeChat history and genuine curiosity close a $1M distribution deal in 6 months because she made her Chinese partners feel respected and understood.

Knowledge of China helps. But trust is what opens doors.

And trust, the kind that matters in Chinese business — comes from three things: showing up consistently, delivering what you say, and making the other person look good in front of their own team.

That last one is underrated. When you help your Chinese partner succeed internally, they will protect you externally. That's the reciprocity logic.

You don't need to be in China to do any of these three things.

* * *

### \[Final Thoughts\]

"Work with China" was never really a question of location.

It was always a question of relationship quality. The most valuable cross-cultural professionals I know aren't defined by their passport stamps or their Mandarin score — they're defined by the depth and durability of their China connections.

The geography will take care of itself once the relationship is real. If you're waiting to "go to China" before you start building, you may be waiting too long.
